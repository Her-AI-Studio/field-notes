Three days on the trail, from Elk Garden to Whitetop Mountain, provided an excellent cross-section of the complex geological history of the southern Appalachians.

## Day 1: Elk Garden to Mount Rogers

The trail ascends through a sequence of metamorphic rocks that tell a story of ancient mountain building. The dominant lithology is a muscovite-biotite schist, with prominent foliation striking approximately 045° and dipping 35° to the southeast.

**Key observation:** At mile 2.3, I encountered an outcrop of quartzite containing what appear to be ripple marks — sedimentary structures preserved despite the intense metamorphism. This suggests a shallow marine origin for the protolith.

## Day 2: Mount Rogers Summit Area

The summit of Mount Rogers (5,729 ft) is underlain by the Mount Rogers Formation — a sequence of rhyolite and basalt flows that represent a period of volcanic activity during the late Proterozoic, approximately 750 million years ago.

> Standing on a billion-year-old volcanic flow, surrounded by the sounds of birds and wind in the trees, one feels the deep time of this landscape. The mountains we see today are but the eroded roots of ranges that once rivaled the Himalayas.

I collected several samples of the rhyolite, which exhibits a distinctive pinkish-gray color with visible quartz phenocrysts. The flow banding is subtle but discernible on fresh surfaces.

## Day 3: Whitetop Mountain

The descent from Whitetop revealed excellent exposures of the Chilhowee Group — a sequence of clastic sedimentary rocks deposited along the margin of the ancient Iapetus Ocean.

### Notable Formations Encountered:

| Formation | Lithology | Age |
|-----------|-----------|-----|
| Erwin Formation | Quartzite, sandstone | Early Cambrian |
| Hampton Formation | Shale, siltstone | Early Cambrian |
| Unicoi Formation | Arkose, conglomerate | Late Proterozoic |

## Mineral Occurrences

I noted several small quartz veins throughout the traverse, particularly in the schistose units. Most were barren, but one vein at approximately mile 8.7 contained small (1-2 mm) crystals of pyrite, weathered to a rusty brown on the surface.

## Glacial Features

While the southern Appalachians were not glaciated during the Pleistocene, periglacial features are present. I observed several areas of solifluction lobes and patterned ground on the upper slopes of Mount Rogers, likely formed during the last glacial maximum when the region experienced tundra-like conditions.
